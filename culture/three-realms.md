# The Three Realms: Heaven, Earth, and the Office Below

Zhong Kui is the Supreme Judge of the Underworld. So why does he spend the game personally chasing ghosts with a sword, instead of sitting at a desk ruling on them?

Why does a game's afterlife have paperwork — bridges to cross, cases to review, queues to stand in?

And why can a god who has lived ten thousand years be *demoted*?

None of this is a design invention. It comes from a specific, very old idea about how the cosmos is administered.

This page is built in three layers. **Layer 1** is what you see on screen. **Layer 2** connects those pieces into one model. **Layer 3** shows where the model came from. Every topic has folded panels underneath — open the ones you want.

## Layer 2 · How They Fit Together

### 1. The Three Realms are not three worlds. They are three jurisdictions

Heaven above, the human world in the middle, the underworld below. It is tempting to read this as three separate settings with different art direction.

It is not. The three realms are **stacked jurisdictions with different laws and constant traffic between them**. Things go up (prayers, reports, promoted mortals). Things come down (edicts, exiled gods, criminals). Almost every plot in Chinese myth begins when something crosses a boundary it should not have crossed.

That is why "the world is out of balance" is such a common premise in these games. The imbalance is not mystical. It is administrative.

::: details Open: Why the human realm is the interesting one

If heaven is order and the underworld is judgment, the human world is the *contested middle* — the place where both jurisdictions reach in and neither fully wins.

That is also where the player lives, which is not a coincidence. A game needs a space where rules can be broken. Heaven and the underworld are both places where the rules already work.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

Early Chinese belief placed the dead under Mount Tai (泰山), governed by a lord known as the 泰山府君. Han-dynasty tomb texts already describe a bureaucracy of the dead, with registers and officials.

Buddhism arrived carrying its own cosmology — hells, rebirth, Yama — and rather than replacing the native system, the two merged. The result is a hybrid: an Indian judge sitting inside a Chinese ministry.
:::

### 2. The Heavenly Court is a government, not a paradise

This is the single most useful thing to understand about Chinese cosmology, and it is the thing Western players most often get wrong.

The Heavenly Court has ministries, censors, ranks, salaries, and endless protocol. Its gods hold **positions**. They can be fined, demoted, exiled, or transferred. They have superiors and they have paperwork.

Its most striking feature is that it is boring *on purpose*. Order is not a side effect of heaven. Order is what heaven is for.

::: details Open: Why "boring" is the design goal

Western heaven tends to be depicted as fulfilment — the end of striving. Chinese heaven is depicted as a functioning institution, which means it has all the problems of one: turf wars, favouritism, corruption, laziness.

This is why the Monkey King story works. Sun Wukong does not rebel against goodness. He rebels against *HR*. His crime is refusing a job title he considers beneath him. The comedy only lands because the reader recognises the office.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

*Journey to the West* (16th c.) is the text that fixed the popular image of the Jade Emperor's court as a functioning bureaucracy — with a stable of heavenly horses, a peach orchard with a manager, and gods who get reassigned when they fail.

But the bureaucratic instinct is much older. Imperial China ran on examinations and appointments, and its cosmology was built by the same imagination that built its civil service.
:::

### 3. The underworld's horror is not darkness. It is accuracy

Walk into a game's underworld and you expect dread: fire, screaming, suffering.

The Chinese underworld is unsettling in a completely different register. It is quiet, procedural, and — worst of all — **fair**. There is a ledger. The ledger is not wrong. The file on you is accurate, including the parts you had convinced yourself did not count.

That is why 判官 is such a compelling figure and why Zhong Kui has lasted a thousand years. The fantasy on offer is not power. It is a court that actually reads the documents.

::: details Open: Why the clerks are scarier than the monsters

Monsters can be fought. A clerk with a correct file cannot.

This is the engine underneath a great deal of Chinese ghost fiction: the horror is not that the system is cruel, but that the system is *right*, and you have no argument. The protagonist's only move is to be more honest than the record — or to find the one error in it.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

*Taiping Guangji* (太平广记, 10th c.), the Song anthology of Tang tales, is full of underworld trials: an ordinary person dies, is brought before a tribunal, is shown their own register, and discovers a detail they had forgotten.

*Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* (聊斋志异, 18th c.) later made the local version of this a household genre — city gods and judges presiding over small, human cases.
:::

### 4. Death is a procedure, not a wall

In a Western framework, death is the end of the story or the beginning of a different one. In the Chinese framework, death is a **step in a process** with stages, checkpoints, and staff.

There is a bridge. There is a queue. There is a clerk. There is, at the exit, a bowl of soup.

This has a direct consequence for game design that is easy to miss: **a world where death is procedural is a world where the dead can be helped, bribed, appealed to, or rescued.** Death becomes an adventure location rather than a fail state.

::: details Open: Why this is why Chinese games can kill a character and keep going

If death is a wall, killing the protagonist ends the story.

If death is a procedure, killing the protagonist *relocates* the story. The hero can negotiate with the underworld, break out of it, be sent back, or discover that the paperwork was wrong. The narrative keeps moving through the boundary rather than stopping at it.

This is also why resurrection in these stories rarely feels like a cheat. It was never forbidden — it was just expensive.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The systematisation of the ten courts of the underworld (十殿阎王) developed from the Tang into the Song, laid out in texts such as the *Sutra of the Ten Kings* (佛说十王经).

Each court handles a stage of review, with its own magistrate and its own punishments. It is a court system drawn as architecture — and it is why the underworld in these games so often looks like an office complex that happens to be underground.
:::

### 5. Crossing the line is the engine of nearly every story

Put the previous four together and the pattern falls out.

Chinese myth rarely generates plot from "a hero wants power" or "a villain wants destruction". It generates plot from **a boundary being crossed**: a mortal steals the elixir, a monk's disciple steals the peaches, a ghost escapes the register, a god is reassigned to a job he does not accept, a demon cultivates past its station.

The Three Realms exist to be stable. The story exists because something destabilised them. Which is why the resolution is almost never "kill the bad guy" — it is "put the world back into a working order".

---

### The thesis

**Chinese myth is not about how powerful the gods are. It is about how order runs — and who repairs it when it breaks.**

That is the reason Zhong Kui, the Judge, and the City God matter more in these stories than the strongest warrior in heaven. They are not the ones with the most power. They are the ones who maintain the system.

And it is why Zhong Kui chases ghosts personally instead of ruling on them from a desk: the desk is already working. Something else has gone wrong.

## Layer 3 · Where All Of This Came From

| Concept | Key text | Era | What it contributed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The dead under Mount Tai | Han tomb texts; 泰山府君 tradition | Han | The native idea of a governed afterlife |
| Hells and rebirth | Buddhist sutras, *Sutra of the Ten Kings* | Tang–Song | Yama and the ten courts |
| Underworld trials as literature | *Taiping Guangji* | Song (Tang tales) | The register, the tribunal, the forgotten detail |
| Heaven as an office | *Journey to the West* | Ming | Ranks, promotions, exiles, stolen peaches |
| Local gods doing small cases | *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* | Qing | City gods and judges with a human caseload |

This layer is the line between this site and a wiki. We are not trying to lay out all of Chinese cosmology — it does not agree with itself, and the disagreement is the interesting part.

We only want you to know one thing: every "lore" you meet in a game has a real text behind it, with a date, an author, and an argument attached. The designers did not make this up. They read it.
