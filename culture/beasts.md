# Beasts & Myths: Why Chinese Monsters Are Researched, Not Invented

Play a Western fantasy game and you can often name the design conversation behind each monster: this one is a dragon variant, that one is a zombie with a twist, the boss is a spider because spiders read well at that scale.

Play a Chinese one and the feeling is different. The bosses feel **cited**.

Why is a monster sometimes just a face, with no body at all?

Why does one of the most famous creatures in the culture only show up when the government is doing well?

And why do the art books keep referencing a book that was already ancient two thousand years ago?

This page is built in three layers. **Layer 1** is what you see on screen. **Layer 2** connects those pieces into one model. **Layer 3** shows where the model came from. Every topic has folded panels underneath — open the ones you want.

## Layer 2 · How They Fit Together

### 1. The Classic of Mountains and Seas is a geography, not a mythology

The most useful thing to know about 山海经 is what kind of book it is.

It is not a scripture and not an epic. It is a **geography** — a survey of the world as it was imagined, moving region by region, listing what lives there, what it looks like, what it eats, and what happens if you eat it. Some of it is recognisably real fauna. Some of it is a mountain with two heads.

That format is why Chinese monster design feels like research. When a designer needs a creature, they are not inventing one from a mood board. They are **looking one up in a survey** — and the survey has an index, variants across editions, and two thousand years of people arguing about what a given entry means.

::: details Open: What a "geography" implies that a "mythology" does not

A mythology explains origins and gods. A geography **assigns locations**.

That is why Chinese creatures come with addresses. The nine-tailed fox is from the east. The taotie is on the bronze. The qilin appears where governance is good. Every creature is somewhere, which means every creature can be *found* — which is exactly what an open-world game needs.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (山海经) was composed in layers: an older core of mountain surveys, expanded across the Warring States and stabilised in the Han, ending at eighteen chapters.

It was not treated as fiction. It was treated as a **reference work**, which is why it kept being annotated, corrected, and argued over rather than retold.
:::

### 2. Chinese monsters are cultivated, not born

This is the single biggest difference between a Chinese bestiary and a Western one, and it changes what a monster can be in a story.

A Western monster usually has a nature. A demon is a demon; it does what its kind does. A Chinese 妖 is an **outcome**. Any animal, plant, or even object that has cultivated long enough — often by sitting in the mountains for centuries — can gain awareness and a form.

Which means a yao has no fixed alignment. It is what its cultivation made it, and what people did to it.

::: details Open: Why this is why Chinese villains get backstories

If a monster's nature is fixed, a backstory is decoration. If a monster's nature is the *result of a process*, the backstory is the mechanism.

That is why the antagonist in a Chinese game so often gets a long, sympathetic treatment: the designer is not softening an evil creature. They are **showing you the cultivation** — how it got here, from what, and because of whom.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The idea of cultivation (修行) as a route to transforming your own kind comes from the Taoist tradition as much as the Buddhist one — see [The Taoism Code](/culture/taoism) on internal alchemy.

Its most influential literary form is *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* (聊斋志异, 18th c.), where foxes are not monsters but characters: proud, petty, generous, vengeful, and morally legible. The book's whole argument is that a yao's ethics are earned.
:::

### 3. Divine beasts are omens, not power levels

A Western dragon is a power fantasy. Its function is to be an obstacle and a reward.

The 麒麟 does not do that. A qilin appears **when the realm is well governed**. It hurts nothing, deliberately — tradition says it walks without treading on living grass. It is not strong. It is *significant*.

This is a completely different job description, and it explains something that confuses Western players: why so many Chinese mythic creatures are non-combatants. They are not there to be fought. They are there as **signal** — the world telling you the state it is in.

::: details Open: Why omens make better worldbuilding than stats

A creature that signals the state of the world can be placed anywhere, at any scale, without needing a fight attached.

Put a qilin in a village and you have told the player the region is at peace — cheaply, wordlessly, and without a quest marker. Put a taotie on a gate and you have told them what the people inside fear. That is a very high information-to-cost ratio.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The omen tradition runs through the *Classic of Documents* and the imperial memorial system alike: unusual creatures, weather, and celestial events were recorded because they were **read** — as reports on the quality of governance.

The qilin's most famous appearance is the capture of one during a hunt in 481 BCE — the 西狩獲麟 recorded in the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, after which Confucius is said to have stopped writing. An omen that lands at the wrong time is not a blessing. It is a report.
:::

### 4. A bestiary can be a character

Bai Ze knows the true name, form, and counter of every spirit in existence.

Notice what that is: **the index, personified**. Bai Ze is not a fighter. Bai Ze is a database with a face, and a book is named after it — the *Bai Ze Tu* (白泽图), a manual of how to identify and deal with spirits.

Zhong Kui is sometimes painted riding one. A judge, with an index.

::: details Open: Why "the catalogue is a character" is a real design move

Once the index is a character, you can put it in a scene.

That is exactly what these games do with the talking-guide figure: a creature that names things for you, which is both a gameplay convenience and a very old literary device. The bestiary is not a menu outside the fiction. It is *in* the fiction, and it has opinions.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The *Bai Ze Tu* (白泽图) circulated as a practical spirit-manual in the medieval period — a list of what exists and how to handle it.

This is the same instinct that produced the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*: a culture that responded to an unknowable world by **writing an inventory of it**.
:::

### 5. The designer cites; the designer does not invent

Put the four together and you get the distinctive texture of Chinese monster design.

The creatures come from a survey with addresses. They have natures that are earned rather than assigned. Their non-combatants are omens rather than obstacles. And the culture already has a mythologised index of the whole thing.

So when a game puts a boss in front of you, the design move is usually **translation, not invention**: which entry, which edition, which detail was everyone arguing about — and what does it look like when it moves.

---

### The thesis

**In the world of the Classic of Mountains and Seas, monsters are not enemies. They are part of the world. You fight one because it is in the way — not because it is evil.**

That is why so much of Chinese creature design carries an unmistakable sense of *provenance*. It is not a style choice. It is the format of the source material, carried forward for two thousand years into a medium that happened to need exactly that kind of book.

## Layer 3 · Where All Of This Came From

| Concept | Key text | Era | What it contributed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A survey of the imagined world | *Classic of Mountains and Seas* | Warring States–Han | The shared bestiary, with addresses |
| The first systematic commentary | Guo Pu, *Classic of Mountains and Seas* annotated | Eastern Jin | These creatures explained rather than retold |
| Naming and classifying things | *Erya* | Warring States–Han | The tradition of getting names right |
| How to handle a spirit | *Bai Ze Tu* | medieval | The index as a usable object |
| Beasts with ethics | *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* | Qing | The yao as a moral character, not a monster |

This layer is the line between this site and a wiki. We are not going to catalogue all of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* — it has three hundred-odd entries, and people have been arguing about them for two millennia.

We only want you to know one thing: every "lore" you meet in a game has a real text behind it, with a date, an author, and an argument attached. The designers did not make this up. They read it.
