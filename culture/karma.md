# Samsara & Karma: Nobody Gets To Win And Be Finished

Western games kill the protagonist and the story ends. Chinese games kill the protagonist and the story keeps going — often as if nothing had happened.

"When this is over, you'll pay for it" is a standard threat in any language. But in Chinese stories it is often not a threat at all. It is a **statement of fact**, with a schedule attached.

And in more than a few of these games, the final reveal is that the villain and the hero are the same person, on different trips around the same loop.

None of this is a twist for its own sake. It comes from a model of cause and effect that is genuinely different from the Western one.

This page is built in three layers. **Layer 1** is what you see on screen. **Layer 2** connects those pieces into one model. **Layer 3** shows where the model came from. Every topic has folded panels underneath — open the ones you want.

## Layer 2 · How They Fit Together

### 1. Death is a checkpoint, not a wall

This is the structural difference, and everything else follows from it.

A Western story usually treats death as a boundary condition: you cross it and you are no longer in the story. A Chinese story treats death as **a step in a cycle** — one you will come back from, changed, owing something, or being owed.

The design consequence is enormous. If death is a wall, killing a character is a full stop. If death is a checkpoint, killing a character is a **scene transition**. You can kill the protagonist on page one and still be telling the same story on page three.

::: details Open: Why "restart from death" is thematically cheap here

In a Western framework, resurrection needs justification — a miracle, a rule broken, a cost paid. It always reads as an exception.

In a cyclical framework, coming back is not exceptional. It is the default. The interesting question is never "how did they come back" but **"what did they come back as, and what do they still owe"**.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The twelve-linked chain of dependent origination (十二因缘) in the *Āgamas* describes existence as a cycle with no first cause and no final exit except enlightenment — birth, ageing, death, and back.

Buddhism brought this to China, where it merged with native ideas about the governed afterlife (Module 02). The result is the popular cosmology these games draw on: not purely Buddhist, not purely native, but the working hybrid.
:::

### 2. Karma is a ledger, not a verdict

The most common mistranslation of 因果 is "punishment". That gets the mechanism backwards.

Karma is not something a judge hands down. There is no sentencing hearing. The ledger **balances itself** — cause produces effect the way weight produces pressure, without anyone deciding to make it so.

A verdict can be appealed, bribed, or overturned by someone more powerful. A ledger cannot. Its horror is exactly the horror of the underworld's files in Module 02: nobody is persecuting you. The books are simply correct.

::: details Open: Why this makes better villains than "evil"

A villain who is evil needs a motive, and motives are easy to argue with.

A debt does not need a motive. A character who is paying something back — or collecting something owed — can be sympathetic, monstrous, and completely understandable all at once, without the writer having to justify anything. They are not choosing to do this. They are **settling an account**.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

The *Taiping Guangji* (太平广记) devotes whole categories to 报应 — tales of retribution — collected from Tang sources. They are formulaic on purpose: a cruelty is committed, and then, sometimes decades later, the account closes.

This is where karma stops being philosophy and becomes **plot machinery**. The delay is the point; it is what makes the eventual settlement feel like fate rather than coincidence.
:::

### 3. Why the bill does not arrive immediately

This is the piece most people miss, and it is the one that answers the most common objection to karma: if the world balances its books, why do bad people prosper?

The answer was worked out in China in the 4th century, by the monk Huiyuan, in an essay called *On the Three Retributions* (三报论).

Retribution comes in three timings: **in this life** (现报), **in the next life** (生报), or **in some later life** (后报). The ledger is not late. It is simply not on your schedule.

::: details Open: Why this is a design gift for storytellers

Once retribution can land in a later life, you unlock the single most powerful motive in Chinese narrative: **a debt you did not personally incur**.

A character can be born into the consequences of something an ancestor did, or spend a life resolving something they only half remember. That is a motive structure the West mostly does not have, and it is why these games can make "I owe you" carry weight across an entire plot.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

Huiyuan (慧远, 334–416) wrote the *Sanbaolun* to answer a specific criticism: that Buddhism's promise of justice was obviously false, since the wicked thrive and the good suffer.

His reply — that the accounting is real but the settlement period is longer than a human lifespan — became the standard Chinese answer, and it is the reason "karma" in Chinese fiction so often feels delayed rather than cancelled.
:::

### 4. Forgetting is a mercy and a destruction at the same time

At the exit of the underworld there is a bridge, and on the bridge there is an old woman, and she is holding a bowl.

Drink it and the previous life is erased. You are reborn clean, with no grief and no grudge. Refuse, and you carry the whole thing into the next life as an open wound.

This is the oldest unresolved argument in the culture, and it is staged as a choice at a checkpoint. Is forgetting a kindness, or is it the destruction of a self? The two readings are both fully supported by the same scene.

::: details Open: Why the choice matters more than the answer

Note what the scene actually does structurally: it gives a character **one decision that defines the next life**.

That is an unusually clean piece of narrative engineering, and games borrow it constantly. A protagonist with no memory is not a cliché here — it is a character who made a choice at the bridge, and the story is about finding out which one.
:::

::: details Open: Where the idea actually comes from

Meng Po and the River of Forgetting are late arrivals to the cosmology — they became standard fixtures in the folk underworld of the Ming and Qing, and were fixed in the popular imagination through ghost stories and *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio*.

The detail that matters is that they were added. Earlier underworlds processed the dead without erasing them. The soup is a narrative invention, and a very good one.
:::

### 5. Debts cross lives, which is why nobody gets to be finished

Put the four together and the narrative structure falls out.

If death is a checkpoint, and the ledger balances itself without appeal, and settlement can be deferred by lifetimes, and memory can be carried or erased — then **no story ever really closes**.

A victory in this framework is not the cancellation of a debt. It is a payment on it. Which is exactly why these games can end with the hero winning and still feel unfinished, and why a sequel does not feel like a retcon.

---

### The thesis

**In a world of karma, nobody wins and then is finished. You either pay, or you become the person someone else pays.**

That is the engine under *Black Myth: Wukong* and *Phantom Blade Zero* alike. The protagonist is not chasing victory. The protagonist is chasing **settlement** — and the difference between those two goals is the difference between a Western revenge story and a Chinese one.
